---
title: FIRE Does Not Make You FREE
date: 2026-10-19T20:31:37.938Z

![](/assets/images/free.png)

Imagine waking up on a Monday morning and realising you don’t have to go to work.

No meetings to attend. No emails to clear. No boss asking for an update. No need to drag yourself out of bed because you have to earn a living.

You can get up whenever you like, go wherever you want and spend your time doing things that make you happy. And the best part? You don’t have to worry about running out of money.

Sounds wonderful, doesn’t it?

That is a big part of the appeal of FIRE — Financial Independence, Retire Early. Work hard, save aggressively, invest wisely, build enough wealth, and eventually reach the point where work becomes a choice rather than a necessity.

For many people, that is a worthwhile goal. Having enough money to leave a job you dislike, spend more time with your family or pursue something you have always wanted to do can make a real difference to your life.

But there is something about this picture that deserves a closer look.

***The Promise***

We spend a lot of time working out how much money we need to stop working. We may spend much less time thinking about what life will actually look like when we get there.

I know this feeling from personal experience.

When I first started working, I was a complete workaholic. Starting work early and getting home past midnight was nothing unusual. I didn’t mind the long hours. I was ambitious, I wanted to get ahead, and I believed that if I worked hard enough and proved myself, the opportunities would come and the money would follow. And they did.

I got the promotions. I was given bigger responsibilities and more opportunities to prove myself. On paper, things were going pretty much as I had hoped. On the other side of the equation I was really frugal, often opting to pack lunch from home, keeping my expenses to a minimum. I had a tiny wardrobe of work wear and footwear and wore my clothes till the fabric was close to giving out. And when all your time was consumed by work, there was little of anything else that stood out.

But somewhere along the way, I began to notice something else. I was so caught up with work that I rarely had time for anything else. When a promotion came along, there might be a celebration or a feast, but deep down inside I didn’t always have someone close to me with whom I could truly share the joy. On the contrary, the higher my profile, the higher my salary and the greater my responsibilities, the less I seemed to enjoy myself.

I had been working towards a better position and better pay, believing they would make life better. And in many ways, they did. But when I eventually reached what I thought was a pretty good position, with a salary I was happy with, I found myself in a place I hadn’t quite expected. An emotionally detached and sometimes lonely place.

Looking back, I don’t think my ambition was wrong. There was nothing wrong with wanting to do well, being recognised for my work or taking on greater challenges. I had achieved many of the things I set out to achieve. What I hadn’t fully appreciated was what I might be giving up along the way. And I didn’t really understand that getting what you want and enjoying what you have are not necessarily the same thing.

***We covet what we lack***

It is easy to understand why this happens. When something is in short supply, we tend to imagine how much better life would be if we had more of it. This is the fundamental appeal of scarcity. We covet that which we do not have.

When we are busy working, free time becomes precious. We dream of travelling whenever we want, spending our afternoons reading or simply doing nothing without worrying about work the next morning.

When we can’t afford the fancy cars and big houses and wonderful gadgets, we envy those who can.

The problem is not in wanting these things; it is when pursuing them becomes the whole purpose of our being.

Take holidays, for example. Being able to travel whenever we want sounds wonderful. But having the money and the time to go wherever we like whenever we like may not be that appealing if our partner, family or friends are not there to do it with us.

Of course, we can travel alone. Some people love it. Others may find it less enjoyable than they expected. None of this means that travel or leisure will inevitably become less enjoyable when we have more time.

It simply means that the enjoyment we get from them often depends on more than just our ability to afford them or fit them into our schedules. It may depend on who we share them with, whether we have something to look forward to, or how these activities fit into the rest of our lives.

When we imagine a better future, we often focus on what we want more of. We don’t always think about what else we need to make that future enjoyable.

***There is always a bigger number***

How much is enough, anyway?

If we are going to work towards financial independence, we need to know how much money we require. There is nothing wrong with calculating our expenses, planning for inflation and working out whether our savings and investments can support us.

But the calculation can only be as good as the life we are planning for.

Someone who wants a simple lifestyle close to home will need a different amount from someone who dreams of travelling the world. Someone supporting ageing parents or children may have very different obligations from someone without those commitments.

Even our own needs can change over time.

The number matters, but it doesn’t tell us whether the life behind it is really what we want.

And when the goal is framed around a number, it is easy to keep moving the goalposts. A little more money would make us safer. A bigger portfolio would give us more choices. Another few years of work would make retirement more comfortable.

There is nothing wrong with wanting greater security. The question is whether we are making deliberate choices about what is enough, or simply getting used to wanting more. There is always a bigger number ahead of the one we have.

***There is no future without the present***

“There is no future without the present” Wow! This sounds like some time paradox tagline. But the truth is that while we work towards a future in which we have more freedom, we are also living through the years it takes to get there.

A young couple might postpone travelling or starting a family because they want to reach their financial target sooner. Parents might spend more years working than they would like, telling themselves that the sacrifice will pay off later. Someone approaching their sixties might keep pushing towards a bigger nest egg, even though they already have enough to make some meaningful changes to their life.

These are not necessarily wrong decisions. Sometimes, a period of sacrifice is exactly what is needed to build security and open up future choices.

But the future is not guaranteed to unfold according to our plans. Our health may change. Our family circumstances may change. The people we want to spend time with may not always be available when we are.

Some experiences have their own timing. Quality time with ageing parents cannot always be postponed. Our children will not suspend their growing up and formative experiences until we can find time for them. Nor can we assume that the people we want to share our future with will always be there to join us when we are ready.

This does not mean we should spend our days with reckless abandon. It means that while the future deserves preparation, the present deserves consideration too.

The aim should not be to make every decision around tomorrow at the expense of today. It should be to make conscious trade-offs, knowing what we are gaining and what we are giving up.

***Agency***

Money matters. It gives us security, helps us cope with uncertainty and expands what we can do. Having enough money can mean being able to leave a bad job, care for a loved one, take a break when we need one or pursue an interest without worrying about every dollar spent. These are real benefits.

Financial independence can be an important step towards a better life.  Money gives us options and, more importantly, agency: the ability to make our own choices, act on them and take responsibility for the consequences.

But while money gives us options, it does not automatically tell us which options to choose, help us build the relationships we want or guarantee that the choices we make will bring us fulfilment. We need to understand what matters to us, not just what other people consider successful.

We need the confidence and capability to make choices, rather than simply the financial means to make them. And we need to think about how we will spend our time, who we want to spend it with, and what will give us a sense of purpose. In other words, the goal is bigger than reaching a point where we no longer have to work for money. It is about having the resources, freedom and readiness to live well. Financial freedom is only the beginning.

What if, instead of starting with how much money we need to retire, we started by thinking about the life we want to live? What would a good life look like for us? What would we want to spend our time doing? Which relationships would we want to nurture? What would give us a sense of purpose? What would we regret not having done?

Once we have a clearer picture, we can work backwards.

***Where do we go from here***

What financial resources would we need? What choices could we make today to give ourselves more flexibility tomorrow? What skills, relationships and interests should we develop now, rather than waiting until we have more time? There is no universal number that guarantees a fulfilling life, just as there is no single definition of success that works for everyone. The answer will differ from person to person. Any number that we come up with can be superseded by a bigger number.

FIRE can be part of the answer. For some, it may be the most important part. But it is worth making sure we are not so focused on securing our future that we overlook what we want that future to look like. FIRE sets a target but not necessarily a desirable outcome.

Because the point of having more time and money cannot simply be to have more time and money. It has to be the ability to do something worthwhile with them.

That is the thinking behind being **FREE: Financially Ready, Empowered and Engaged.**

It is not about rejecting FIRE, nor is it about suggesting that money does not matter. It is about looking beyond the financial milestone to the life that milestone is supposed to make possible.

We should absolutely plan for financial security. But we should also give thought to the relationships we want, the experiences we value, the contribution we hope to make and the person we want to become.

After all, the purpose of financial freedom is not merely to free ourselves from work. We should be **Financially Ready** to support the life we want, **Empowered** to make choices that reflect our own priorities and **Engaged** in relationships, activities and pursuits that give life meaning.

To be **FREE** is to give ourselves more freedom to live a life worth living.
